On one L4, the engine mattered less than the memory budget

I ran 46 benchmarks of Qwen3-8B on vLLM and SGLang. FP8 and prefix caching moved latency and throughput more than the choice of engine did. Here is what held up, and what I could not prove.

The short version

  • The engines match until memory fills. Up to 16 concurrent requests, throughput is within 4.2%. At 64, p95 time to first token (TTFT) is 388 ms on vLLM and 21.3 s on SGLang BF16. SGLang's KV pool held 12,505 tokens against vLLM's 27,024 and the memory settings differed, so the gap tracks capacity, not an engine verdict.
  • FP8 weights are a speed lever. On vLLM, W8A8 cut decode time per output token (TPOT) 42.8% (67.2 to 38.5 ms) and raised output 1.7×.
  • FP8 KV cache is a capacity lever. It changed throughput by about 3% or less up to 32 concurrent requests. At 64 it added 19% on vLLM and 45% on SGLang.
  • RadixAttention pays off on shared prefixes. With an 81% shared system prompt it cut p95 TTFT 13.4× (17.3 s to 1.3 s). This is a best case.
  • FP8 did not measurably change GSM8K accuracy. BF16 scored 89.7%, and the FP8 settings 89.3% to 90.5%, all inside the 95% interval of about ±1.6 points.

How I measured

Every run used one NVIDIA L4 (24 GB, Ada sm89, about 300 GB/s by spec) in a Lightning AI Studio, serving Qwen/Qwen3-8B in BF16 or the official FP8 checkpoint (block-wise W8A8). A custom async load tester sent requests to /v1/chat/completions in closed loop, with 2 warmup requests and max_tokens at 256, and took token counts from the server's usage report. There were three workloads:

  • Static: 10 concurrent requests, 100 ShareGPT prompts (mean 229 tokens, median 42, p95 1,459).
  • Prefix cache: the same prompts behind a shared 986-token system prompt (mean 1,217 tokens).
  • Sweep: 1 to 128 concurrent requests in doubling steps, 60 seconds per level, for vLLM and SGLang in BF16 and with FP8 KV cache. Prompts are short (mean 53 to 63 tokens, median 25, checked at 1, 64 and 128 concurrent requests) and every request generates 256 tokens. Up to 32 concurrent requests each level completed 4× its concurrency in requests; at 64 and 128 the count varied with engine speed (172 to 328).

Noise floor

Two independent runs of the same vLLM configuration (a 2,048-token chunk is already the V1 default) agreed on throughput (0.57 req/s), TPOT (67.25 vs 67.39 ms) and p95 inter-token latency (ITL, 68.7 vs 69.1 ms), but TTFT moved about 30% (p50 342 vs 240 ms). So at 10 concurrent requests I trust throughput, TPOT and ITL, and treat TTFT differences under 30% as noise.

Finding 1: the engines match until KV-cache memory runs out

Up to 16 concurrent requests the engines are within 4.2% on throughput (0.95 vs 0.91 req/s), and p95 TTFT is level at 8 (199 vs 193 ms). The curves split at 16, where p95 TTFT is 217 ms on vLLM BF16 and 836 ms on SGLang BF16. At 64 it is 388 ms against 21.3 s.

The same data as a capacity answer: the highest tested concurrency whose p95 TTFT stayed under a limit, and the throughput there.

Why capacity is the likely cause

A sweep request needs roughly 313 KV tokens at full length (its prompt plus up to 256 generated tokens). The startup logs give each pool:

SGLang BF1612,50540climbs from 16, collapses at 64
SGLang FP8 KV25,010801.4 s at 64, collapses at 128
vLLM BF1627,02486388 ms at 64, collapses at 128
vLLM FP8 KV47,456150384 ms at 64, 21.9 s at 128 (see open items)

The FP8 KV cache doubled SGLang's pool exactly and grew vLLM's by 1.76×. For SGLang that matches the result: its 500 ms limit moved from 8 to 32 concurrent requests. vLLM's BF16 pool is 2.2× SGLang's, which fits the gap at 64. This is a likely explanation, not proof: SGLang's CUDA graphs and chunk size also differ.

The KV-cache arithmetic, and decode scaling

Qwen3-8B stores 36 layers × 8 KV heads × 128 head size × 2 (K and V) × 2 bytes = 147,456 bytes, about 147.5 KB, per token in BF16. The SGLang log confirms the pool: #tokens: 12505, K size: 0.86 GB, V size: 0.86 GB. FP8 halves the footprint to about 73.7 KB per token.

On decode: from 1 to 16 concurrent requests p95 TPOT rises only about 10% (60.1 to 65.7 ms on vLLM, 61.5 to 67.5 ms on SGLang), because at small batch sizes decode is limited by memory bandwidth and streaming about 15 GB of weights costs the same for 1 token or 16. From 16 to 128 it climbs to 80 to 88 ms. SGLang BF16 dips from 83.5 ms at 64 to 80.0 ms at 128, most likely because extra requests wait in the queue instead of joining the decode batch.

Finding 2: FP8 weights buy speed, FP8 KV cache buys capacity

The two settings address different bottlenecks.

  • Weights: on vLLM at 10 concurrent requests, the FP8 checkpoint cut TPOT from 67.25 to 38.47 ms (−42.8%) and raised output from 144.5 to 245.9 tok/s (1.70×).
  • KV cache: at 10 requests it changed neither TPOT nor throughput, because they hold only about 4,800 KV tokens. It pays off once BF16 runs out of pool. At 64 requests it raised throughput 19% on vLLM (2.73 to 3.25 req/s) and 45% on SGLang (2.13 to 3.09). At 128 it raised SGLang 62% and vLLM 2%.

A bandwidth check on the 1.7×

Each decode step reads the weights once: about 15 GB in BF16 and 8.2 GB in FP8 (my estimates from parameter counts). At the L4's 300 GB/s spec that sets a TPOT floor of about 50 ms and 27 ms. vLLM sustains roughly 70 to 75% of that bandwidth in both precisions. Halving the bytes gave 1.70× instead of 2×, most likely because fixed per-step costs (kernel launches, attention, activation scaling) take a larger share of a 38.5 ms step.

The SGLang W8A8 run is the exception. TPOT rose to 72.3 ms and GPU utilization fell from 98.4% to 53.5%. It ran with --disable-cuda-graph and logged a warning that no tuned block-FP8 kernel config exists for this GPU. My working explanation is CPU-side launch overhead, which I have not tested. Until a rerun with CUDA graphs enabled does, treat it as an untuned configuration, not SGLang's FP8 ceiling.

Finding 3: RadixAttention pays off when prompts share a prefix

To test it on a RAG-style workload, I put a 986-token system prompt in front of the same 100 user turns. I measured the shared share from the server's usage reports: 81.04% of the mean prompt (95.8% at the median), with only 0.3% overlap among the user turns, so nearly all cache hits come from the shared template.

The most likely reason p95 TTFT hit 17.3 s with the cache off is KV-pool pressure. Ten requests of about 1,217 prompt tokens plus up to 256 output tokens need about 14,730 KV tokens, more than SGLang's 12,505-token pool, so some wait for admission. With the cache on, the shared prefix is stored once, saving about 8,874 tokens.

This is a best case. A RAG pipeline or agent with a fixed template looks like this run. A chat service where every prompt is unique would see close to no benefit. Each setting is a single run of 100 requests.

Finding 4: structured decoding taxes every token, and small prefill chunks hurt the tail

Structured output

Enforcing a JSON schema on SGLang (xgrammar backend) raised mean TPOT 21.6% (69.9 to 85.0 ms), p95 ITL 1.97× and p99 ITL 3.75× (134.0 to 502.7 ms), and cut raw generation speed 24.8% (137.9 to 103.6 tok/s). Request throughput still rose 4.15× and median end-to-end latency fell 5.3×, but that is an output-length effect: unconstrained responses averaged about 251 tokens and JSON-constrained ones stopped at about 45.

Prefill chunk size on vLLM: 512 vs 2,048

Throughput (0.56 vs 0.57 req/s), TTFT and p95 ITL (68.6 vs 69.1 ms) are the same. p99 ITL is 130.9 ms at 512 against 74.5 ms at 2,048 and 71.1 ms in the baseline replicate, so the 512 tail is about 1.8× worse. A likely reason is that long prompts split across 3 to 5 scheduler steps, so more decode steps share a batch with prefill work. I did not test that, and each size is a single run, so this shows 512 was worse here, not that 2,048 is optimal.

Finding 5: FP8 did not measurably change accuracy

I ran GSM8K (all 1,319 test questions, 5-shot, greedy) through lm-evaluation-harness against the vLLM server in each configuration. Scores are percent correct with the flexible-extract filter; strict-match tells the same story.

Every gap is smaller than the interval and no paired test is close to significant, so the +0.8 points for the combined setting is noise, not an improvement. What is visible is churn: even FP8 KV alone changed the answer on 58 of 1,319 questions (4.4%), 29 each way.

What this does not show. It is one benchmark, one seed and greedy decoding. GSM8K is short-context math, so it says little about long-context retrieval, code or multilingual quality, where an FP8 KV cache is more likely to hurt. Read it as "no measurable drop on GSM8K", not "no quality loss".

What did not hold up, and what is still open

  • Small TTFT gains at 10 concurrent requests are noise. Two identical vLLM runs differed by about 30%, so a result like the 22.5% p95 drop from adding FP8 KV (1,385 to 1,073 ms) cannot be separated from arrival-order variance. I do not claim it.
  • The baseline p50 TTFT gap is real but unexplained. vLLM's p50 is 38.6% lower (342 vs 557 ms), beyond the noise band, while p95, TPOT and throughput are level. One untested candidate is that the median 42-token prompt fits vLLM's CUDA graph capture range while SGLang ran without graphs. The launch flags differ in other ways too, so I make no causal claim.
  • An unresolved 11.9-second pause in the SGLang W8A8 run. p95 and p99 TTFT (11,921.11 and 11,921.72 ms) differ by 0.61 ms, so at least 5 or 6 requests got their first token at the same instant: one server-wide stall, not queuing. The log shows a Triton kernel compiling after serving started, but I have not tied it to the pause. The next run did not show it, and one clean run does not show that the KV flag fixed anything.
  • SGLang ran with CUDA graphs disabled in every run. Reruns with graphs enabled are next, and they may change the SGLang decode and W8A8 results.
  • The 128-user point is not explained by KV capacity alone. vLLM with FP8 KV cache has a pool for about 150 requests and 256 allowed sequences, yet its p95 TTFT at 128 is still 21.9 s. Those levels also ran only about 2 to 2.6 waves of requests in 60 seconds, so the first burst may weigh heavily. I have not ruled out a limit in my load generator, so the conclusions above rest on 64 concurrent requests and below.
  • Scope. One GPU (L4), one dense 8B model, GSM8K as the only quality check, and one run per setting except where noted. Higher-bandwidth GPUs or mixture-of-experts models would shift the balance between compute and bandwidth limits.

Reproduce it

The raw JSON for every run, the server logs, the manifest and the plotting scripts are in the GitHub repository. python3 analysis/analyze.py regenerates summary.csv and every figure from the raw JSON.

Commands for the accuracy check and the bandwidth chart 
# accuracy: start the vLLM server in one configuration, then
lm_eval --model local-completions \
  --model_args model=Qwen/Qwen3-8B,base_url=http://localhost:8000/v1/completions,tokenizer=Qwen/Qwen3-8B,num_concurrent=16,max_retries=3 \
  --tasks gsm8k --num_fewshot 5 \
  --gen_kwargs temperature=0,max_gen_toks=512 \
  --seed 0 --log_samples --output_path results/acc_bf16

# bandwidth chart with your own numbers instead of the spec and the estimates
BW_GBS=<measured GB/s> WEIGHTS_BF16_GB=<n> WEIGHTS_W8A8_GB=<n> python3 analysis/analyze.py
